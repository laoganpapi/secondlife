# Contract drafting

## Drafting sequence

1. Extract the deal terms: exact party names (entity type, jurisdiction of formation), consideration, dates, deliverables, special conditions. A missing term becomes an open issue, never an invention; conflicting sources get a question, never a silent merge.
2. Lay the skeleton: title and parties, recitals, definitions, operative clauses, risk allocation, term and termination, boilerplate, signature blocks, schedules.
3. Draft operative clauses first — they carry the deal — then risk allocation as one system (below).
4. Build definitions from the terms the draft actually uses; then recitals and boilerplate.
5. Walk the deal's life — performance, payment, breach, termination, post-termination survival; a scenario the draft is silent on gets drafted or listed as open.
6. Number every section; re-check every cross-reference after any renumbering.
7. Add the DRAFT banner and open-issues list; run the pre-delivery checks.

An amendment names the underlying agreement and its date, quotes each amended section, and states that all other terms remain in force. A standalone clause skips skeleton steps that do not apply but still gets the DRAFT banner, defined-term discipline, and an open-issues list.

## Definitions discipline

- One term, one meaning, one definition. A concept used twice or more gets a defined term.
- Use a defined term exactly as defined, capitalized, character for character: "the Services", never "the work" or "the engagement". Elegant variation is how ambiguity disputes start.
- Definitions describe; operative clauses oblige. No obligation hides inside a definition.
- Every capitalized term is defined and every defined term used; an orphan either way is a defect.

## Operative-clause precision

| Clause type | Signal language | Effect if it fails |
|---|---|---|
| Covenant (promise of conduct) | "shall" or "will" — one verb, held through the whole document | Breach; contract damages |
| Representation | "represents that" plus a present or past fact | Misrepresentation claim — possible rescission, reliance damages |
| Warranty | "warrants that" | Breach-of-warranty contract claim — expectation damages, no reliance needed |
| Condition precedent | Explicit drafting: "It is a condition to [Party]'s obligation under Section X that ..." | Obligation never arises (unless also drafted as a covenant, or the invoking party caused the failure) |
| Right | "may" | None; permission unexercised |

- The choice between "represents", "warrants", or both is a negotiated, jurisdiction-sensitive remedy decision — when the term sheet is silent, flag "[jurisdiction-specific — attorney to confirm]".
- Avoid "provided that" as condition language — courts have read provisos as conditions, covenants, or exceptions depending on context.
- Never blur these types: mislabeling one as another changes the remedy for breach. State amounts, dates, percentages, and party names exactly as given, never rounded or inferred; a missing figure becomes "[AMOUNT — confirm]" plus an open-issues entry.

## Risk-allocation architecture

Warranties, indemnification, and limitation of liability are one machine; draft them together.

- Express warranties: what is promised about the goods, services, or stated facts, and for how long; check that no later disclaimer erases them.
- Implied warranties: decide expressly whether implied warranties (merchantability, fitness for purpose) are given or disclaimed; disclaimers have jurisdiction-specific formal requirements — flag "[jurisdiction-specific — attorney to confirm]".
- Indemnities: who pays for defined losses, typically third-party claims, with trigger, scope, and claims procedure stated.
- Limitation of liability: the cap, the consequential-loss exclusion, and written-out carve-outs (commonly fraud, willful misconduct, indemnity obligations).
- Interaction check: does the cap cover the indemnities? Are warranty claims inside the exclusion? State the answers expressly.

## Load-bearing boilerplate

| Clause | Failure it prevents |
|---|---|
| Governing law and forum | The dispute starts with a fight over where to fight |
| Assignment | The contract is assigned to a third party without consent. Ownership change is different: protecting against it needs an express change-of-control provision, a separate negotiated term |
| Notices | A valid termination notice reaches nobody binding |
| Entire agreement | Prior drafts leak back in as terms. Excluding reliance on precontractual statements needs an express non-reliance clause; fraud cannot be excluded — "[jurisdiction-specific — attorney to confirm]" |
| Amendment and waiver | Informal emails or a course of conduct silently vary the contract or waive termination and payment rights |
| Severability | Reduces the risk that one unenforceable clause takes related terms or the deal with it. Check it against clauses the client would not want the contract to survive without |

## DRAFT banner and open issues

- First line of every output: **DRAFT — for attorney review, not agreed.**
- Last section: "Open issues" — each business decision the drafter cannot make (cap amount, indemnity scope, term length, governing law) as a one-line question. Never resolve one with a silent "reasonable" default.

## Pre-delivery checks

- Defined terms: each defined once, used verbatim, no orphans.
- Cross-references: each pointer lands on the section it names.
- One obligation verb throughout.
- Amounts, dates, and party names match the source character for character.
- Cap, indemnity, and warranty interaction stated expressly.
- DRAFT banner present; open-issues list complete.

## Competence boundary

- The output is language, not legal advice. A licensed attorney must review every draft before execution.
- Jurisdiction-specific rules (non-compete enforceability, consumer statutes, notarization, execution formalities) get flagged "[jurisdiction-specific — attorney to confirm]", never guessed.
- Never claim a draft is enforceable.
