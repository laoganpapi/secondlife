# Customer discovery

## Three rules

1. Talk about their life, not your idea.
2. Ask about specifics in the past, never opinions about the future.
3. Talk less, listen more — the customer speaks 80%+ of the time.

## Question design

- Facts live in past behavior; compliments live in hypotheticals. Banned: "Would you use X?", "Would you pay for X?", "Is this a good idea?" — everyone lies to be nice.
- Openers that work: "Talk me through the last time this happened." "What are you using now?" "What have you already tried?" "What does this problem cost you in money or hours?" "Who else should I talk to?"
- Feature requests are never taken literally — they are data about a problem, not a spec. Dig: "Why do you want that? What would it let you do? How are you coping without it?"

## No pitching

- The moment the idea is described, the interview becomes a sales call and every later answer becomes politeness. The solution stays unmentioned until the problem conversation is exhausted.
- Anything said after a pivot into "let me show you" is contaminated — mark it as such in notes.
- Catching yourself explaining or defending means learning has stopped.

## Commitment and advancement

Words are not evidence; cost is. A good meeting ends with the prospect giving up something valuable:

| Currency | Examples |
|---|---|
| Time | Scheduled follow-up with an agenda, a working session, a trial they'll actually run |
| Reputation | Intros to peers, intro to the budget-holder, public reference |
| Money | Letter of intent, pre-order, deposit, signed pilot |

- "Sounds great, keep me posted" is a polite rejection.
- If you don't know what happens next after the meeting, the meeting was pointless.
- The awkward ask for commitment is the measurement instrument — skipping it voids the interview.

## Sample and saturation

- Recruit people who recently lived the problem — not friends, not solution enthusiasts.
- Segment tightly first ("heads of ops at 20–200-person logistics firms who hired last quarter"). Contradiction across ten interviews usually means the segment is too broad: slice and re-run.
- Batches of 5–10 per segment; stop at the learning plateau where answers become predictable — typically 10–20 for a first pass. Saturation is the stop rule, not a magic number.

## Notes and synthesis

- Exact quotes, recorded with consent — paraphrase smuggles in bias.
- Tag each note: emotion shown, money mentioned, workaround described, commitment offered.
- Synthesize within 24 hours; cluster across interviews by problem, current solution, cost, and trigger event.
- Raw notes go to the whole team — one person monopolizing interviews is a single point of interpretation failure.

## Validation standard

A problem counts as validated only when multiple independent people in the same segment:

1. Describe the same problem unprompted
2. Have actively tried to solve it — built a spreadsheet, paid for a workaround, assigned headcount
3. Advance with real commitment when offered a next step

Pain with no attempted solution is usually a problem people can live with. Active workaround-seeking is the strongest pre-product evidence.

## Failure modes

Leading questions · pitching disguised as interviewing · feature requests taken literally · compliments counted as demand · interviewing whoever is easiest · fishing for validation after a lukewarm answer · skipping the commitment ask
