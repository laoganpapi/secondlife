# Competitive analysis

## The real competitive set

- Start from the buyer's view, never a category list. Three tiers: direct (same solution, same job), indirect (different solution, same job — a spreadsheet, an agency, an intern), and status quo.
- The deciding question: what would customers use if you didn't exist? For most early products the biggest competitor is non-consumption — muddling through with email and Excel.
- An analysis that omits "do nothing" overstates win rate and misdiagnoses losses.

## Collection set per competitor

| Item | Where the truth lives |
|---|---|
| Positioning — claimed category, claimed audience | Their homepage |
| Segment actually served | Their case studies, not their homepage |
| Pricing and packaging — tiers, value metric, discount behavior | Pricing page diffs over time, deal anecdotes |
| Go-to-market motion | Self-serve flow vs. "book a demo"; job postings |
| Trajectory — funding, headcount growth, release velocity | Raises, careers page, changelog |

Trajectory beats snapshot: a weaker product shipping fast is more dangerous than a stronger one stalled.

## Feature-table traps

- A grid you build shows you winning — you chose the rows.
- Buyers weigh outcomes and buy for one or two reasons, not feature counts.
- Use feature tables only as sales enablement against a named competitor, never as the strategic picture.

## Moat assessment

For each serious competitor, name which durable power they hold and which you could build (Helmer's seven): scale economies · network economies · counter-positioning (a model incumbents can't copy without harming their existing business) · switching costs · branding · cornered resource · process power.

- Assess power relative to each competitor, including potential entrants.
- A startup's only available early power is usually counter-positioning — name yours explicitly or admit you have none yet. That admission is a finding.

## Win/loss interviews

The highest-signal input in the whole discipline:

- Interview actual buyers within 2–4 weeks of decision — wins and losses both. Why you win matters even more than why you lose; it's the raw material of positioning.
- Interview the buyer, never your own rep — reps systematically report "price" for losses that were really fit or trust.
- Each win reason maps to a differentiator to lead with; each loss reason maps to a counter or a qualify-out rule.
- A dozen honest win/loss interviews beat any amount of desk research.

## Living document

- A competitive deck decays in a quarter. Assign an owner, a standing intake (win/loss notes, pricing-page diffs, release notes, job postings), and a review cadence tied to planning.
- Date every claim — a stale "fact" repeated in a sales call costs credibility.

## Honest 2x2s

- Axes chosen before plotting, from dimensions buyers actually decide on — found in win/loss data.
- Every placement defensible with evidence.
- The vendor magically alone in the top-right is the tell that axes were reverse-engineered. If an honest 2x2 doesn't flatter you, that is the finding: differentiation unproven.

## Failure modes

Feature-parity roadmapping instead of widening the gap that wins deals · ignoring non-consumption · stale decks as current truth · analysis produced once for a board meeting · tracking the feared big name instead of the competitor in actual deals · sales anecdotes substituting for buyer interviews
