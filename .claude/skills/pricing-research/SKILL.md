# Pricing research

## Price comes early

- Most product failures are monetization failures decided long before launch: the product gets designed, then someone asks what to charge. Reverse it — have the willingness-to-pay conversation during discovery so price shapes what gets built.
<!-- predelivery-allow: hidden gem -->
- The four failure types this prevents: feature shock (overloaded product nobody values whole), minivation (right product, priced too timidly), hidden gem (valuable capability never monetized), undead (product nobody would pay for, shipped anyway).
- Order of decisions: value metric and packaging early; price level last.

## Methods ladder

Cheapest to heaviest — climb only as far as the decision's stakes justify:

1. **Direct conversation.** "Would you pay $X?" is a trap — politeness plus anchoring. Ask instead: what do you spend today on this problem; what would you compare this to when justifying the spend; then the acceptable / expensive / prohibitive sequence, reading reactions over numbers.
2. **Van Westendorp.** Four questions: too cheap to trust · a bargain · getting expensive · too expensive to buy. Plot cumulative curves; the intersections bound the acceptable range.
   - Read the range, never the point: the "optimal price point" is conventional, not truth to the dollar.
   - Needs ~100+ respondents per segment who understand the product.
   - Weak for feature-rich or head-to-head competitive decisions; fine for a first corridor.
3. **MaxDiff.** Pick most/least valued from rotating feature sets — yields a clean value ranking that ratings can't (everyone rates everything "important"). Sorts features into leaders (drive purchase), fillers, and killers (features some segments refuse to fund) — which drives good/better/best packaging.
4. **Conjoint.** Choose among full product profiles with prices; estimates willingness to pay per attribute and simulates share. Use when the decision is big enough to fund it.
5. **Purchase-intent scales.** Only top-box ("definitely would buy") means anything, and practice halves even that.
6. **Revealed preference — the gold standard you already own.** Won/lost deals by price point, discount depth by segment, churn after price changes, upgrade rates between tiers. Deep discounting concentrated in one segment is measured willingness to pay, not anecdote. Real price tests and actual pre-orders outrank every survey.

## Value metric

- Ask what unit customers would prefer to pay per — seats, usage, transactions, outcomes.
- Three tests: it tracks value received; it's easy to measure and predict; it grows as the customer succeeds.
- A wrong value metric caps revenue at any price level. Interview for the metric before setting the number.

## Research to decision

- Never average willingness to pay across segments — a one-size price overcharges half the market away and undercharges the rest. Spread in WTP means segmentation and tiers, not a mean.
- Triangulate at least two methods (Van Westendorp corridor + discount-ledger data is the workhorse pair) before committing.
- Deliverable shape: a price corridor per segment plus a recommended structure — metric, tiers, fences — with behavioral evidence ranked above stated evidence.

## Failure modes

Believing "yes" to "would you pay $X" · averaging across segments · cost-plus pricing that never asks WTP (and its cousin: matching a competitor who also guessed) · pricing research done once at launch · Van Westendorp's crossing point treated as truth · features ranked by unforced ratings instead of trade-offs · the discount ledger — the richest WTP dataset most companies own — never read
