# Investment memo form

The shape to write against for an investment memo, diligence pack, or company brief. Derived
from a sample Alex supplied as a model. Load when writing one; skip otherwise.

The sample earned its status on substance and register. Its structure was the one weak part,
and that part is corrected here rather than reproduced.

## Section skeleton

Eleven sections, this order. Drop one only when the subject genuinely has nothing to put in
it, and say so in a line rather than omitting the heading silently.

| Section | Its job | Shape |
|---|---|---|
| Company Snapshot | What the company is, in a form someone can repeat after one read | Short opener, then a labelled fact block |
| Disclaimer | Where the analysis stops and what a reader may not rely on | Prose |
| Product | What is sold and how it is built | Sub-headed, bullets per capability |
| Business Model | How money is made, and how that departs from the industry default | Contrast first, then revenue lines as bullets |
| Market Opportunity | Who the customers are and why revenue per customer is high | Prose for the argument, bullets for the market components |
| Timeline of Milestones | What has actually happened, dated | One bullet per date |
| Team | Who is accountable and what they have done before | One bullet per person |
| Competition | Who else is in the field, grouped by kind | Sub-head per group, bullets per competitor |
| Financials | What the numbers are and how much weight they bear | Provenance note first, then bullets |
| Risks | What an investor is underwriting | One labelled bullet per risk |
| Recent News | What changed lately, with sources | One bullet per item, reverse chronological |

## Conventions that carry

- **Labelled bullet, three parts.** Every enumerated item runs **name** → mechanism →
  consequence: what it is, why it happens, what it does to the reader's decision. A bare
  label with no mechanism is a heading pretending to be analysis; a mechanism with no
  consequence leaves the reader to guess why they were told.
- **Provenance stated per section, not buried.** Any section resting on estimates opens by
  saying so — what the figures are drawn from, which are audited, which are projections. A
  reader who learns this in a footnote has already formed a view on bad terms.
- **Sources attached to the item, not the document.** News and third-party claims carry their
  source on the same line. A bibliography at the end cannot be checked against a specific
  claim, so it will not be.
- **Facts flat, projections marked.** State what is true without hedging. Mark what is
  expected with "is expected to" or "could", every time. Mixing the two registers makes the
  whole document read as promotional, and the reader discounts the facts along with the
  guesses.
- **Approximation shown, not implied.** `~$4.4B` and "approximately" do work that a bare
  rounded figure does not: they tell the reader the precision on offer.
- **Name the real entities.** Name the actual competitors, regulators, and investors, prior
  failures in the space included. "Leading investors" and "regulatory bodies" are the writing
  equivalent of a blank space, and the reader treats them as one.
- **Negatives stated plainly.** Loss-making, capital-intensive, concentrated, cyclical — say
  it in the ordinary word. A memo that never says anything unwelcome is not read as optimistic,
  it is read as unreliable.
- **Contrast before claim.** A section explaining how something differs opens with the thing
  it differs from. The reader cannot hold "different" without the baseline.

## What to fix from the sample

Its enumerating sections were written as continuous prose. In four of them the author had
already typed the labels — dates in the timeline, names in the team, dated entries with
sources in the news — and then run them together. The list existed; only the line breaks were
missing.

- **Anything with a repeating label is already a list.** If items begin with a date, a name, or
  a category followed by a colon, they are bullets. This is mechanical, so there is no
  judgement call to get wrong.
- **Four or more parallel items in a comma-series is a list**, where the items are things a
  reader may need to find again, compare, or count. The sample buried runs of five and seven
  capabilities inside single sentences. A short series naming categories in passing is not
  caught by this — breaking four words onto their own lines costs more than it returns.
- **One section per idea, and the enumerating ones do not get prose.** In the sample a single
  section used bullets properly. Where one section in a document is structured and its
  neighbours are not, the neighbours are the defect.

Target sentence length is 20 to 25 words on average. The sample averaged 34, with 15% over
40, which is where the density is felt even after the lists are broken out.

## Before delivering

1. Every enumerating section is bulleted, and every bullet has all three parts.
2. Every figure that is an estimate is marked as one, in the section that carries it.
3. Every third-party claim names its source on the same line.
4. No comma-series of four or more parallel items survives anywhere.
5. Ends on the last substantive point — no summary, no sign-off (writing-standards, Formatting 4).
